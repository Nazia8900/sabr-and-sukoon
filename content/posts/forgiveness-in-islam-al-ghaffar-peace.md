Grudges vs. Al-Ghaffar: Why Holding On Hurts You More Than Them
By The Sukoon Seeker · Sabr and Sukoon · Updated July 2026 · 11 min read
📌 In Short:
Forgiveness in Islam isn't weakness — it's one of the highest spiritual acts a Muslim can perform, reflecting Allah's own name Al-Ghaffar. Research on forgiveness independently confirms what this practice has always pointed to: releasing resentment measurably reduces stress and improves wellbeing.
What Forgiveness Actually Means in Islam
Maafi is not passivity. It is not pretending you weren't hurt.
It is the deliberate act of releasing resentment, choosing peace over the pull of pain, and trusting Allah with justice instead of carrying that weight yourself.
وَأَن تَعْفُوا۟ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ
"But if you pardon, overlook, and forgive — their reward is with Allah."
— Surah At-Taghabun (64:14)
Notice what this verse does not say. It doesn't say the reward is with the person you forgave, or that you must wait for them to deserve it. The reward is with Allah — meaning forgiveness costs you nothing you actually keep by withholding it.
Allah Is Al-Ghaffar — The Constant Forgiver
One of Allah's own names is Al-Ghaffar — the One who forgives repeatedly, completely, without limit. When you're asked to forgive, you're being invited to reflect this attribute back into the world.
"Allah will not show mercy to one who does not show mercy to people."
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7376
Forgiveness in Islam is not only about the person you release. It's about who you become in the process, and the mercy you unlock for yourself in return.
The Conquest of Makkah: Forgiveness at Maximum Power
The clearest example of this in the Seerah is the conquest of Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ stood before the very people who had tortured his companions, killed those he loved, and driven him from his home.
"No blame will there be upon you today. Go — for you are free."
— recorded in the Seerah
This wasn't resignation. It was forgiveness chosen deliberately, at the exact moment he had full power to do otherwise. Psychologists have a term for this kind of response — unconditional positive regard, considered one of the most healing postures a person can hold toward another. The Prophet ﷺ modeled it 1,400 years before that term existed.
What Research Independently Confirms
Dr. Frederic Luskin's Stanford Forgiveness Project has studied this question directly for years. Across multiple studies, including work with mothers who lost children to political violence in Northern Ireland, participants who underwent forgiveness-focused training showed measurable decreases in depression, anger, and stress-related symptoms — not just self-reported relief, but documented change.
Separately, researchers describe unforgiveness — sustained grudges and resentment — as keeping the body in a prolonged fight-or-flight state, with elevated cortisol and reduced immune function. Forgiveness has been shown to reverse this: activating brain regions tied to empathy while reducing activity in areas linked to rumination and negative emotion.
وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ عَلَىٰ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا
"And do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just."
— Surah Al-Ma'idah (5:8)
The Quran named this danger — resentment clouding judgment — centuries before neuroscience could measure exactly how.
5 Practical Steps to Actually Practice This
1. Remember your own need for forgiveness first.
The Prophet ﷺ made istighfar more than 70 times daily. If the best of creation needed constant forgiveness, this softens the ground for extending it to others.
2. Make dua for the person who hurt you.
"The dua of a Muslim for his brother in his absence is answered" (Sahih Muslim). This shifts you from victim to agent — a change compassion-focused therapy approaches independently arrive at too.
3. Separate forgiveness from reconciliation.
Forgiving doesn't require trusting them again or continuing a harmful relationship. It's an internal release for your own healing — boundaries and forgiveness coexist in Islam.
4. Use istighfar to soften reactivity.
When resentment rises, pause and say Astaghfirullah slowly. This isn't just seeking forgiveness — it's a heart-softening practice that creates room for genuine release.
5. Remember the Akhirah is watching.
A narration in Ibn Abi Ad-Dunya's collection describes those who forgave being called forward on the Day of Resurrection to receive their reward — a reminder that this act is never wasted, even when it feels unseen now.

Final Sukoon Reflection
Holding a grudge feels like protection. It rarely is. What it actually does is keep you tethered to the moment that hurt you, long after the person who caused it has moved on with their life. Al-Ghaffar is not asking you to forget, or to pretend it didn't happen. He's offering you a way to put it down — and trusting Him with what's left.

Frequently Asked Questions
What does forgiveness in Islam mean?
It means releasing resentment toward someone who wronged you, choosing peace over pain, and trusting Allah with justice — reflecting His own attribute Al-Ghaffar, with documented benefits for mental and physical wellbeing.
Does forgiving someone mean accepting what they did was okay?
No. Forgiveness separates the internal act of releasing resentment from condoning wrong behavior. You can fully forgive while still maintaining boundaries and seeking justice where appropriate.
How did Prophet Muhammad ﷺ practice forgiveness?
Most notably at the conquest of Makkah, forgiving those who had persecuted him and his companions for years, saying: "No blame will there be upon you today. Go, for you are free."
