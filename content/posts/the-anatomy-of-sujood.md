The Anatomy of Sujood: What Science Confirms About Salah's Physical Benefits
By
Nazia Firdous (The Sukoon Seeker)
· Sabr and Sukoon · 9 min read
In Short:
What happens to your spine, your muscles, and your circulation when you go down in sujood? Recent research has begun looking at the physical effects of the positions of salah. This article reviews what modern research actually says — and why worship always comes first.
There is a quiet, steady movement in the daily life of a Muslim — the movement of salah. Every time you stand, bow, prostrate, and rise, your body moves through a sequence that has been performed for over 1,400 years. That sequence is not random. Every position has a purpose.
Modern research has started to examine the physical benefits of these postures — not to argue that salah is a form of exercise, but to understand what the movement might be doing for the body. The findings are, in their own way, worth reflecting on. Because while scholars have long described sujood as the position in which a person is closest to Allah, researchers are now describing it as one of the most biomechanically significant movements in the entire prayer sequence.
And here, when we say "research," we're drawing on peer-reviewed studies, not speculative articles. As
the peace that comes from letting go of comparison
is a spiritual matter, so too is the wisdom in what was commanded — and what was commanded was never arbitrary.
The Hadith That Still Stands
"The closest a servant comes to his Lord is when he is prostrating."
— Sahih Muslim (482)
Salah is worship before it is anything else. We perform it because it is commanded, not because we are seeking a physiological outcome. The Prophet ﷺ described prostration as the moment of greatest closeness to Allah — and that spiritual nearness remains the primary significance of the movement. What we do with our bodies is important, but it is important because it is commanded.
That said, there is nothing wrong with noticing that the movement also happens to be biomechanically sound. The body, after all, was created with a purpose. And the movements prescribed by the Creator might reasonably be expected to align with how the body was designed to move.
Some readers may recall the du'a of the Prophet ﷺ in sujood: "O Allah, forgive me all my sins, the small and the great, the first and the last, the public and the private." This is not a request for physical healing; it is a plea for spiritual mercy. And yet, physical healing sometimes follows spiritual closeness as a secondary mercy, not the primary one.

What the Research Says About Each Position
1. Qiyam (Standing)
Standing in salah involves maintaining an upright posture with arms at the sides or folded over the chest. It is a position of attentive stillness, not physical exertion. Researchers have noted that standing in salah encourages awareness of posture, gently reinforcing spinal alignment without requiring any unusual effort.
2. Ruku' (Bowing)
Bowing involves bending at the waist while keeping the back straight and hands resting on the knees. This movement stretches the lower back and the hamstrings — the muscles along the back of the thighs. A 2022 review paper by Sarkingobir and colleagues synthesized findings from multiple studies, noting that while research on prayer postures is still emerging, the existing evidence consistently points to low-risk, positive physical effects, particularly for maintaining flexibility in the spine.
However, the evidence on ruku' specifically is limited. Most of the research on salah postures has focused on the more pronounced movements — particularly sujood.
3. Sujood (Prostration)
Sujood is the most physically distinctive movement in salah. Seven points touch the ground at once: the forehead, the nose, both hands, both knees, and the toes. It is a position of complete submission — and biomechanically, it is also a position that places the head below the heart, which may promote blood flow to the brain and facial tissues.
Khasawneh and colleagues (2018) found that the combination of flexion and weight distribution in sujood may help maintain healthy spinal curvature and reduce lower back tension, particularly when performed correctly. The same study noted that the position of the spine in sujood falls well within the safety thresholds established by NIOSH (the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) for low-risk movement. There is simply no evidence of harm in healthy individuals.
But here we also need to be honest: the evidence on sujood is preliminary. The Khasawneh study was limited by a relatively small sample size, and the research on the health effects of Islamic prayer is still limited compared to research on other forms of physical movement. A 2022 review by Sarkingobir and colleagues noted that while many studies report positive effects, the overall quality of evidence remains limited, and most are cross-sectional rather than long-term clinical trials.
In other words: the research is interesting, but it is not yet conclusive. It supports reasonable reflection — but not a claim that sujood is a proven medical intervention.
4. Juloos (Sitting)
Between the two prostrations, the sitting posture provides a moment of rest. The weight shifts from the hands and knees to the hips and feet. This alternation between kneeling and standing is biomechanically sound because it allows the body to move through a full range of motion without static strain in any one position.
What the Research Does Not Claim
It is important not to overstate what the research says. Scientists have observed certain physical effects of salah postures, but the studies so far are small, preliminary, and limited in scope. There are no large-scale, long-term clinical trials confirming that sujood prevents back pain or circulation problems. The claims made in popular wellness spaces often exceed what the research actually supports.
For example, some websites claim that sujood "increases blood flow to the brain" to the point of improving cognitive function. The actual research is much more modest: it suggests a plausible effect, not a proven one. Similarly, statements about sujood "curing" this or that condition are not supported by peer-reviewed evidence.
If you are experiencing back pain, poor circulation, or any other health concern, please see a qualified healthcare provider. Salah is an act of worship, not a form of physical therapy, and the benefits you might feel are secondary to the spiritual purpose.
What This Means for Your Daily Salah
Focus on sincerity first.
The physical benefits of sujood are secondary to the spiritual nearness to Allah that the Prophet ﷺ described. Your intention matters more than your alignment.
Pay attention to form where possible.
If you can maintain a comfortable posture, do so. If you cannot, due to age, injury, or illness, Islam provides alternatives.
Don't force movements.
If a posture causes pain, consult a doctor. Salah should be a source of peace, not strain.
Make dua in sujood.
The closeness mentioned in Sahih Muslim is the real significance of the position. The physical effects are not the point, but they are a quiet sign of the wisdom behind what was commanded.
Final Sukoon Reflection
When you go down into sujood today, let the physical sensation remind you of the spiritual reality: you are as close to Allah as a person can be. Your forehead touches the ground, your hands and knees bear your weight, and for that moment, you are physically lower than everything around you — but spiritually raised.
The research may or may not confirm what you feel. But the hadith does. And that is enough.

Frequently Asked Questions
Does sujood have scientifically proven health benefits?
Yes. Research suggests sujood may help maintain healthy spinal curvature and reduce lower back tension when performed correctly. However, most studies are limited and findings remain preliminary.
Should I treat sujood as a form of therapy?
No. Sujood is an act of worship, not a medical treatment. It may have physical benefits, but these are secondary to its spiritual purpose.
How do I know if I'm performing sujood correctly for my spine?
Ensure your back is straight, hands flat, forehead and nose touching the ground. If you have back pain or existing spine conditions, please consult a doctor before making any changes.
